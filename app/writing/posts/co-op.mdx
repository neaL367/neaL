From January 5 to April 24, 2026, I spent four months working as a software developer at TQM through my university's Co-operative Education Program. It was my first in-depth experience working on production systems inside an enterprise engineering organization.

Looking back at those four months, the experience fundamentally shifted how I approach software engineering, team collaboration, and personal growth.

## Stepping into the Enterprise

Walking into the office on day one was intimidating. Building hobby projects in isolation is completely different from contributing to a living codebase with real users, business requirements, and established infrastructure.

In the beginning, I was quiet and cautious. But when you are surrounded by complex systems, you learn quickly out of necessity. Step by step, by asking questions and reviewing previous pull requests, I began finding my footing.

## Understanding the Real Software Engineering Lifecycle

Before this internship, my development workflow was simple: write code, run it locally, and push to a repository. At TQM, I experienced the complete end-to-end engineering cycle:

1. **Investigation:** Breaking down business problems and understanding user pain points before writing a single line of code.
2. **Requirements & Design:** Clarifying specifications, defining data contracts, and planning architecture.
3. **Implementation:** Writing clean, maintainable code following team standards.
4. **Automated & Manual Testing:** Ensuring unit tests pass, edge cases are covered, and regressions are caught early.
5. **User Acceptance Testing (UAT):** Validating the solution with stakeholders in staging environments.
6. **Production Deployment & Monitoring:** Shipping the changes safely and verifying health metrics.

Experiencing this workflow firsthand taught me that writing code is often only a third of an engineer's job. Clear communication, thorough testing, and operational discipline are just as critical.

## Engineering Cadence and Daily Standups

Every morning began with a focused 15-minute standup meeting:

- What was completed yesterday
- What is planned for today
- Blockers, bugs, or dependencies requiring cross-team help

These concise syncs kept everyone aligned and created an open environment where asking for assistance was encouraged rather than discouraged.

## Autonomy and Senior Mentorship

What I appreciated most about the engineering culture was the balance of autonomy and guidance. Developers were empowered to choose appropriate tools and patterns to solve problems effectively, keeping the focus on outcomes rather than rigid micro-management.

Whenever I ran into technical hurdles, senior engineers were always willing to dive in. Instead of simply dictating the solution, they walked me through *why* a particular architectural choice made sense in the broader system context. Those code reviews and discussions were masterclasses in software design.

## Teammates and Culture

Navigating high-stakes releases, troubleshooting tricky regressions, and celebrating successful deployments alongside my fellow co-op interns made the journey truly memorable. We shared stress, laughter, and breakthrough moments together.

Four months passed quickly, but the compound learning from real-world software development is unmatched. For any student considering a co-op or internship program: take the leap. The lessons you gain from shipping real software alongside seasoned engineers will accelerate your growth far beyond the classroom.

Thank you to the entire team at TQM for an unforgettable four months.
