This is what I build with. Tools change; the reasons for picking them don't.

**Fast to develop in, reliable enough to leave running, quick for the user, and simple enough that I still understand it in a year.**

## Frameworks

### [Next.js](https://nextjs.org/)

My default for anything full-stack in React. **Server Components mean I ship less JavaScript**, and I can read from a database without building an API layer just to hand data to a page.

## Backend and APIs

### [Node.js & Express](https://expressjs.com/)

For small REST APIs where I don't want much structure imposed on me. **A route is a function, middleware is a function**, and there is nothing else to learn before writing the first endpoint.

### [.NET & ASP.NET Core](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/aspnet/core/)

When the service has to survive real load, or the domain is complicated enough that **I want the compiler on my side**. Good throughput out of the box, dependency injection built in, and async that doesn't misbehave under pressure.

## Styling and Design

### [Tailwind CSS](https://tailwindcss.com/)

I stopped keeping a separate stylesheet. Classes sit next to the markup, and the build **only ships the ones I actually used**, which keeps the bundle from growing quietly.

### [shadcn/ui](https://ui.shadcn.com/)

The components get **copied into the repo instead of installed**. It's Radix underneath, so the keyboard and screen reader behaviour is already handled, and I can change anything without arguing with a library.

## AI

### DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

I use it while working:

- figuring out why something broke
- drafting a type that's annoying to write by hand
- asking whether an approach is a bad idea

**Low latency matters more than raw capability here.** The value is in the fast back and forth, not in a perfect answer.

## Game Development

I'm building an **isometric open-world game with a medieval setting**. It's a long-term project — *years rather than months* — and I picked the toolchain with that in mind.

> The camera never moves. Everything has to read from one direction, and whatever I build once has to keep working somewhere I haven't built yet.

That single constraint decides where the effort goes. Models are low-poly because detail disappears at this zoom, and lighting is tuned for **one view instead of a free camera**. An open world makes the rest harder, since I can't hand-place every corner of the map.

### Unity and URP

[Unity 3D](https://unity.com/) is the engine, running the **Universal Render Pipeline**. The forward renderer lets me keep shadow distance short and choose which objects cast at all. That matters at this camera angle, where a thin shadow turns into noise rather than depth. **I light the scene once**, for the view the player actually gets.

### Blender

Where the models come from. Built so **the outline is recognisable** at the size the game draws it; detail inside that outline mostly goes to waste.

Buildings, terrain and props come out of one set of medieval materials, so I'm not inventing a new look for every region I add. Scale and pivots are set at the source, then exported as `glTF` or `FBX`, so nothing needs fixing again after it lands in Unity.

## Hosting

### Vercel

Where my Next.js apps end up by default. **Preview deployments per branch**, and CI that needs almost no configuration.

### AWS

When a project needs finer control:

- **CloudFront** — low-latency delivery and edge caching worldwide.
- **S3** — durable object storage for uploads and media.
- **EC2** — dedicated servers for custom daemons or specific workloads.

## Community

Outside of code, I run IT workshops and volunteer with programs that teach beginners and underprivileged students. **Explaining a concept to someone new is the quickest way to find the parts of it I never actually understood.**

Pick tools that solve the problem in front of you, that you can still maintain next year, and that get out of the way of shipping.
